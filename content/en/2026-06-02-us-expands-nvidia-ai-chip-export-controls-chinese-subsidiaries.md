# US Closes the Subcontractor Loophole: Nvidia AI Chips Now Blocked From Reaching Chinese Firms Through Overseas Subsidiaries

The US Commerce Department issued sweeping new guidance this week effectively blocking advanced Nvidia AI chips from reaching Chinese companies through overseas subsidiaries and third-party markets. The move closes a loophole that had allowed Chinese AI firms to source cutting-edge American hardware outside China — including through markets like Malaysia — despite formal export restrictions already in place.

The timing is significant: this is no longer about border controls. It's about corporate supply chain controls.

"We are watching where those chips end up, not just where they cross," a Commerce Department official said in a briefing with reporters. "If a Chinese company owns a subsidiary in another country, those chips still cannot be used to advance Chinese AI capabilities."

Nvidia's most advanced chips — including the H100 and the newer Blackwell-generation products — have been subject to export restrictions since 2022. But enforcement focused on direct shipments to China. The new guidance widens the net considerably, targeting the corporate structures and ownership chains that let Chinese firms indirectly access restricted technology.

## The Loophole That Wouldn't Close

Industry analysts had long noted that Chinese AI companies were finding workarounds. Some set up overseas research arms to procure chips legally, then transferred models and knowledge back to the parent company. Others routed purchases through intermediaries in neutral markets. Nvidia has consistently supported compliance with export laws but has also acknowledged the challenge of tracking chips once they're in the wild.

The new rules essentially shift the burden: any chip that could ultimately serve a Chinese entity under common ownership or control structures is now treated as a potential violation. Regulators say they will use customs data, corporate filings, and intelligence sharing with allied governments to enforce the rules.

## Industry Pushback

Some in the semiconductor industry have warned that the expanded controls could accelerate efforts by Chinese firms to develop domestic alternatives — a dynamic that could, over time, weaken the US chip industry's global position. Others argue the risk of simply waiting is greater, as Chinese AI capabilities are advancing on multiple fronts simultaneously.

The new guidance follows a broader pattern: export controls expanding from physical borders to corporate structures, reflecting how global supply chains have become too complex for simple country-of-origin rules to manage.

Nvidia declined to comment specifically on the new guidance, citing the ongoing nature of regulatory developments.
