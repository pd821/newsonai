# An AI Just Proved a Math Theorem Mathematicians Couldn't Crack for 80 Years

A new OpenAI reasoning model has solved a landmark problem in combinatorial geometry — one that eluded mathematicians for nearly eight decades — by deploying tools from algebraic number theory that no one expected to apply to an elementary question about points and distances in the plane.

The problem, posed by Paul Erdős in 1946, asks: if you place *n* points on a flat plane, what's the maximum number of pairs that can sit exactly one unit apart? For almost 80 years, the best constructions relied on square and hexagonal grids. Mathematicians suspected those arrangements were optimal. They were wrong.

OpenAI's model produced an infinite family of point configurations that outperform the square grid by a polynomial margin — a meaningful improvement in a field where progress is measured in fractional gains. The result has been reviewed and verified by external mathematicians, including Fields Medalist Tim Gowers, who called it "a milestone in AI mathematics."

**"Current AI models go beyond just helpers to human mathematicians,"** said number theorist Arul Shankar of the University of Toronto. **"They are capable of having original ingenious ideas, and then carrying them out to fruition."**

The proof itself is notable for its method. Erdős's problem lives in the world of geometry — drawing points and measuring distances. The model solved it by borrowing machinery from algebraic number theory, a branch of mathematics concerned with abstract structures like rings and fields, not shapes on a plane. Applying number theory to a plane geometry problem is an intellectual move that human mathematicians had not tried. It worked.

The model was not trained specifically for mathematics. It is a new general-purpose reasoning system, and this proof emerged from OpenAI's broader effort to test whether advanced models can contribute to frontier research. The chain of thought that led to the proof — including the surprising algebraic number theory bridge — is documented in a companion paper the company released alongside the main result.

The full proof is available on OpenAI's public filing servers, along with a remarks paper and the model's chain-of-thought log. That transparency is unusual in AI research, where model reasoning is often treated as a black box. OpenAI appears to be inviting scrutiny, and mathematicians are taking up the offer.

The result has implications beyond the specific problem. If a general-purpose AI can produce novel, verified mathematical arguments — not just assist human mathematicians by checking work or suggesting next steps, but actually generate the core insight — the role of AI in research changes in a fundamental way.

Whether the model "understands" mathematics is a question the paper does not answer, and philosophers will debate it for some time. What the proof demonstrates, more narrowly, is that the model can extend the boundary of human knowledge in a domain that demands rigor. That boundary, it turns out, was waiting for a new angle of attack — even if the angle came from an unexpected place.
