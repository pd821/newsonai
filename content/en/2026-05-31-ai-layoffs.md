# The AI Layoff Wave: "AI Psychosis" or Smart Business?

**SAN FRANCISCO** — ClickUp, the productivity software company once celebrated for its human-centered culture, announced it was cutting 22% of its workforce this week — attributing the move to a strategic shift toward AI agents. The news landed like a stone in an already roiling pond: tech layoffs in 2026, according to one tracker, are now nearly matching the total for all of 2025.

The result is a debate that is quickly becoming the defining labor story of the AI era: when companies replace human workers with AI systems, is that efficiency — or something more troubling?

## "AI Psychosis"

Box founder Aaron Levie has a term for it. Speaking at a recent industry conference, he described a growing pattern among company leaders — replacing workers with AI tools they don't fully understand — as "AI psychosis."

"The people deciding that AI can replace your job," Levie said, "are also the ones least likely to understand what your job truly involves."

His comments resonated widely, particularly after ClickUp's announcement. On LinkedIn and X, former employees posted about being let go with little explanation beyond "pivot to AI." Several described roles — customer success, documentation, quality assurance — as functions they believed required human judgment and relationship-building.

## The Numbers Don't Lie

Layoffs.fyi, which tracks tech industry cuts, reports that companies have announced more than 180,000 job losses attributed to AI automation in the first five months of 2026. That's on pace to surpass 2025's full-year total, itself a record.

What's different this time: the cuts are concentrated in white-collar knowledge work — roles that were considered relatively safe during previous waves of automation that hit manufacturing and retail harder.

A recent survey by the World Economic Forum found that 44% of tasks performed by knowledge workers today could be automated or augmented by existing AI tools within three years. For some companies, that estimate isn't moving fast enough.

## The Pushback

Not everyone is accepting the narrative quietly. DuckDuckGo, the privacy-focused search engine, has seen a notable uptick in installations in recent months. Its CEO, Gabriel Weinberg, has been vocal about what he describes as a coordinated effort by Google to "force AI into search" at the expense of the clean, link-based experience users originally came for.

"We built DuckDuckGo because we thought search should serve users, not advertisers," Weinberg said in a post that went viral among the tech community. "Now we're watching history repeat with AI — companies that got the basics right are being dragged toward something users never asked for."

## Looking Ahead

The tension isn't going away. Workers are organizing — or retraining. Some are learning to work with AI tools rather than be replaced by them. Others are simply looking for employers who haven't decided the "efficiency" of AI is worth the cost.

For now, the companies pressing ahead say they're being pragmatic. The market, they argue, rewards those who move fastest.

But if Levie's "AI psychosis" framing gains traction, the question may shift from *who* is replacing *whom* to *whether anyone understands what they're actually doing*.
